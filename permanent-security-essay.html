Building the Permanent Peace: Three Sensors Against the Long Catastrophe

Civilisations are not destroyed by the threats they anticipate. They are destroyed by the ones they could have detected, cheaply, at the door — and didn't. A case for three unglamorous instruments, and the centuries of cost they would save.

Every era believes its catastrophes arrive unannounced. They rarely do. The 1918 influenza left fever logs at ports that nobody acted on; the 2011 Fukushima disaster followed decades of published seismic risk that regulators discounted; the 2007 financial crisis was foretold, in outline, by economists whose warnings were filed rather than funded. The pattern is not that humanity fails to see danger coming. It is that humanity persistently underinvests in the unglamorous instruments that would let it see danger coming cheaply enough, early enough, and everywhere at once to matter. This essay is about three such instruments — none of them exotic, all of them presently underbuilt — and about why, viewed across the full length of civilisational time rather than the length of a budget cycle, they are among the best investments a modern state can make.

The case for each rests on the same underlying arithmetic: the price of permanent, distributed sensing is falling every year, while the price of the catastrophes such sensing would prevent or blunt is rising every year, denominated as much in psychological and economic disruption as in the immediate physical toll. Britain's own pandemic bill alone ran to somewhere in the region of £310–410 billion — several thousand pounds for every person in the country — a sum that dwarfs, by orders of magnitude, what distributed detection infrastructure would have cost to install in advance. That asymmetry, once understood, does not describe a single crisis. It describes a design principle for the rest of the century, and arguably for the rest of time: a civilisation that wishes to persist indefinitely must eventually treat detection infrastructure the way it now treats plumbing — not a discretionary purchase, reconsidered project by project, but a permanent, invisible layer beneath ordinary life.

The doorway as border

The first instrument is the least technically settled and the most immediately intuitive: reprogrammable, camera-and-machine-learning pathogen screening, installed at the doorways of public and private buildings the way fire alarms and smoke detectors are today. The instinct behind it is sound — the 2003 SARS outbreak and the COVID-19 pandemic both taught modern states that the border of a country is not really its coastline or its airport immigration hall; it is every threshold a contagious person crosses on an infectious day. But the first generation of doorway screening, thermal-imaging fever cameras rushed into airports and offices from 2003 onward, was a lesson in false confidence rather than a proof of concept: independent studies during the 2009 influenza pandemic found such systems caught as few as one in seventeen infected travellers who crossed the screened border, a sensitivity so poor that the security theatre it produced arguably cost more in complacency than it returned in protection.

The correction is not to abandon the doorway as a sensing surface but to change what it senses for. Newer approaches — acoustic classifiers that listen for the signature of a diseased cough, computer-vision systems that read gait and posture for signs of illness, and airborne biosensors capable of identifying pathogen particles in the surrounding air within minutes — are still largely confined to laboratories, but they point toward something categorically different from a thermal camera: a genuinely reprogrammable sensor, one that can be retasked from influenza to a novel coronavirus to whatever the next pathogen turns out to be, without ripping out the hardware. The economic logic of building this network in advance, rather than improvising it under emergency procurement during the next outbreak — as happened, chaotically and expensively, in 2020 — is straightforward: prevention research of this kind has been costed at a small fraction, on the order of a few percent, of the value of the lives such prevention would save each year, and at a similarly small fraction of what a single serious outbreak costs a modern economy in lockdowns, lost output, and public health response. A state that installs reprogrammable detection at its building doors today is not buying certainty against the next pandemic. It is buying the fastest possible warning of one, at a price that any future finance ministry will look back on as trivial against the quarter-trillion-pound alternative.

A hand on the switch

The second instrument is the most conceptually uncomfortable, because it asks democratic societies to consider building deliberate points of control into the silicon their entire economy runs on. The proposal is to embed governance mechanisms directly into processors — not as an afterthought of software, which can be patched around, but at the level of the transistor itself, where the direction of semiconductor doping determines how a given circuit behaves and can, in principle, be used to implement functions that are extraordinarily difficult to detect or remove without specialised equipment. This is not science fiction: dopant-level circuit modification was demonstrated, as a security research exercise, over a decade ago, and it remains one of the stealthiest known ways to alter what a chip does without altering what it visibly contains.

The reason to take this seriously as policy rather than as a curiosity of hardware security research is the trajectory of machine intelligence itself. States spend enormous sums today insuring against threats with single-digit percentage probabilities and civilisation-scale consequences — nuclear war chief among them. Economists modelling the risk of catastrophic outcomes from advanced, poorly governed artificial intelligence have argued, using the same logic nations already apply to pandemics and nuclear deterrence, that annual spending on the order of a percentage point of GDP would be a rational insurance premium against a risk experts themselves place, in aggregate, in the low single digits. Measured against that benchmark, the entire global field of technical AI-safety research was funded, as of last year, at a level barely exceeding one hundred million pounds worldwide — a rounding error beside the hundreds of billions being spent annually to build the systems such safeguards are meant to constrain. On-chip governance is the cheapest available lever precisely because the industry standard chips already carry most of the necessary security infrastructure — trusted execution environments, secure enclaves, remote attestation — installed for entirely different commercial reasons. The honest case for pursuing this at the transistor level is not that it is elegant; it is that the alternative, discovering after deployment that a runtime "kill switch" implemented purely in software can be bypassed by the very system it was meant to restrain, is a mistake a civilisation only gets to make once. There is a defensive dividend here too: a state that understands how to implant an undetectable hardware control also understands, better than any rival, how to detect one planted against it — turning a controversial capability into a form of supply-chain insurance that pays for itself in counter-espionage alone.

A Geiger counter in every pocket

The third instrument is the most mature and the cheapest to justify. Distributed radiation detection — networks of low-cost gamma and neutron sensors, ultimately extending into consumer electronics themselves — has already moved well past the theoretical stage. A programme run by the American defence research agency DARPA demonstrated, using little more than one hundred networked detectors placed around a single transport hub, more than a hundredfold improvement in the ability to locate and identify a radiation source in an urban environment, and went on to field a thousand such sensors across an entire capital city. The unit economics are, by the standards of national security infrastructure, remarkably ordinary: British-made spectroscopic detectors supplied for that programme were priced at around four hundred dollars apiece at scale, and the solid-state sensing elements that make cruder detection possible cost, at the component level, a matter of a few pounds.

A dirty-bomb detonation in a single financial district has been modelled at close to sixteen billion dollars in economic damage — fuelled overwhelmingly not by the physical blast but by a decade of fear-driven avoidance. A full nuclear detonation at a major port could exceed a trillion. Against costs of that magnitude, a sensor costing a few pounds is not a difficult argument to make.

What makes this instrument suited to the "rest of time" framing more than the other two is that its central vulnerability — nuclear and radiological material moving undetected through ordinary commerce and transport — does not diminish with technological progress; if anything, the number of state and non-state actors capable of acquiring such material only grows across decades. The International Atomic Energy Agency's own incident database has logged several thousand reported cases of radioactive material lost, stolen, or unaccounted for since it began tracking them, more than half of the thefts occurring not in vaults but in ordinary transit. A civilisation that wants nuclear terrorism to remain a live risk it can detect, rather than a live risk it merely hopes not to encounter, needs the sensing surface to be as ordinary and ubiquitous as the smartphone already is. Consumer-grade radiation sensing will not replace dedicated detection networks at ports and borders; it extends their reach into every street, every building lobby, every pocket, at a marginal cost so far below the cost of the disaster it screens for that the comparison barely survives as a comparison.

The arithmetic of forever

None of these three proposals is, on its own, a complete security architecture, and none should be deployed uncritically: the doorway biosurveillance case is the least evidentially mature and should be funded as research with explicit accuracy thresholds before it is mandated anywhere, the chip-level safeguard is a serious enough intervention in the world's computing infrastructure that it should be built defensively and transparently rather than as a unilateral backdoor, and even the best-proven of the three, distributed radiation sensing, still depends on public trust to avoid becoming a surveillance liability rather than a security asset. What unites them is not their technical similarity but their economic shape. Each substitutes a small, permanent, distributed cost for a large, occasional, catastrophic one. Each becomes proportionally cheaper the longer the time horizon under consideration, because silicon and sensors depreciate in price over decades while pandemics, uncontrolled machine intelligence, and nuclear terrorism do not depreciate in consequence.

Foreign policy is usually written in units of years — a parliamentary term, a defence review cycle, a budget. The proposals in this essay only make full sense in units of centuries, because that is the timescale over which their savings compound and their omissions become unrecoverable. A nation that spends a few billion pounds a decade embedding reprogrammable detection into its buildings, its chips, and its consumer electronics is not purchasing safety for the next parliament. It is purchasing the standing option to still be here, doing ordinary things, in the next one — and the one after that, for as long as anyone cares to keep counting.
