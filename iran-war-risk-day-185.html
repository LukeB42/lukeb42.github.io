How Bad Can the Iran War Get?

Six months since Operation Epic Fury, the war hasn't ended and hasn't gone nuclear — it has frozen into a grinding blockade. Here's what actually happened, what's still unresolved, and what the next six months plausibly look like.

Six months ago, on Saturday 28 February 2026, the United States and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury against Iran, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei within hours and igniting the most significant Middle East war since the 2003 Iraq invasion. Since then the conflict has moved through four distinct phases — a brutal five-week opening campaign, a fragile Pakistan-brokered ceasefire and naval blockade, a brief and genuine peace under a US–Iran memorandum in June, and a collapse back into strikes and a sanctions war in July and August. As of today, bombs have not fallen on Iranian soil for roughly a month. But the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed, oil is a third more expensive than before the war, and no one — not CSIS, not Crisis Group, not the belligerents themselves — believes this is over.

This is not the raging, escalating war some feared in March, nor the negotiated peace that briefly seemed within reach in June. It is something more familiar from history and harder to write clean scenarios about: a frozen conflict, sustained by mutual exhaustion and a pivot from bombs to sanctions, that could restart at any provocation and shows no sign of genuinely ending.

Below: where things actually stand after six months, the two questions that matter most for the next six — the nuclear file and Britain's exposure — the full timeline, and an updated decision tree for what happens next.

Essay I

Where Things Actually Stand at Six Months

It helps to think of the war in four phases. Phase one (28 Feb–early April) was the opening decapitation campaign: near-900 US–Israeli strikes in the first 12 hours, Khamenei's death, and Iranian retaliation across the Gulf that hit Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Kuwait and even RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus. Phase two (April–May) was a Pakistan-mediated ceasefire that never really held — Israel kept striking Lebanon within hours of the truce — followed by a US naval blockade of Iran and an Iranian refusal to fully reopen Hormuz, producing what analysts called a "dual blockade." Phase three (mid-June) was the closest the war came to actually ending: a 14-point Islamabad Memorandum, signed by Trump and President Pezeshkian on 17 June, briefly reopened Hormuz toll-free and sent oil crashing back to pre-war levels. Phase four (July–present) is where we are now: Trump declared the memorandum "over" on 8 July, US strikes resumed on dozens of targets, three US troops died in a Jordan base attack, the Houthis reopened a second front in the Red Sea, and Washington pivoted to what Treasury Secretary Bessent calls an "economic D-Day" — sweeping secondary sanctions rather than more bombing.

The Iranian regime did not collapse, but it was decapitated and transformed. Mojtaba Khamenei, the late Supreme Leader's hardline son, was elected by the Assembly of Experts after an internal succession struggle that Israel tried to disrupt by bombing the leadership compound a second time. He ended a week of public silence in late August with a defiant message urging Gulf states to identify their "real enemy." Masoud Pezeshkian remains president, a striking continuity given how much else has changed.

⚠ Casualties Remain Genuinely Contested

Every belligerent has an incentive to shape the numbers. Iranian human-rights monitors put the toll at roughly 3,500–3,600 killed (roughly evenly split between military and civilian); US and Israeli officials have suggested figures above 6,000. The US Defense Department's official count of 18 troops killed is disputed by independent reporting suggesting several hundred total US casualties since fighting resumed in July. Treat any single figure in this space as a claim, not a fact.

The Strait, in Numbers

Hormuz carried roughly a quarter of the world's oil and a fifth of its LNG before the war — about 20 million barrels a day, per US Congressional and IEA figures. Commercial transits collapsed from a pre-war baseline of around 73 vessels a day to single digits at the March low, in what the IEA called the largest energy-supply disruption on record. The June memorandum briefly restored real traffic — the first Saudi tankers in months moved through on 18 June — before the July collapse reimposed the blockade. CENTCOM says it has helped roughly 1,500 vessels through the strait and redirected 82 since mid-July; the IRGC calls that "an obvious lie." The UN's shipping agency reports 6,000 sailors currently stranded in the region and 19 seafarers killed since the war began. In late August, Iran and Oman announced a revenue-sharing arrangement for the strait — the first real structural movement in months — but Tehran is explicit that this is not a reopening, and is holding out for sanctions relief and reparations first.

Why the US Switched from Bombs to Sanctions

The blunt answer, per CSIS defense analysts, is that the US is running low on the exact munitions this kind of war consumes. Estimates suggest Operation Epic Fury's opening campaign alone burned through more than a quarter of the US's pre-war Tomahawk inventory and over half its Patriot interceptor stock, pushing Patriot reserves below 1,000 and THAAD interceptors to roughly 250 — reserves CSIS says could take until 2029–2031 to rebuild, opening what it calls "a window of vulnerability" for any future Pacific contingency. That, as much as diplomacy, is why Bessent's "Operation Economic Outcast" — threatening secondary sanctions on any bank or shipper, including Chinese ones, that touches Iranian oil, shipping, gold or crypto — is now the main US line of effort, at least until after November's midterms.

Essay II

The Nuclear Question, Six Months On

This is the single most important unresolved thread of the entire war, and it has gotten no clearer since March. As of the last confirmed IAEA verification, Iran held 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60% — a hair's breadth, technically, from weapons-grade. The IAEA has now had no access to verify what happened to that material for more than a year, ever since Iran walked away from its cooperation agreement with the agency in November 2025. Iran's foreign minister offered in March to down-blend the stockpile; no verified action has followed.

☢ Nine Weapons' Worth, 99% of the Way There

Arms-control analysts note that Iran's stockpile, if further enriched to 90%, "would provide the fuel for nine nuclear weapons" — and that roughly 99% of the enrichment work needed to get from 60% to 90% has already been done. The mitigating factor US and IAEA assessments keep pointing to is that Iran still appears to lack a proven weapon design and hands-on experience with uranium metal. That is a genuinely important caveat. It is not a permanent one.

US intelligence itself is split on how much damage the war actually did to the program. A low-confidence Defense Intelligence Agency assessment leaked in the spring suggested the strikes set Iran back only months; CIA Director Ratcliffe has publicly claimed years of setback. Both cannot be fully right, and neither can be verified from outside without inspectors back on the ground — which is precisely the problem.

The clearest external signal of how dangerous the overall environment has become is the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists' Doomsday Clock, set on 27 January 2026 — a month before this war even began — at 85 seconds to midnight, the closest it has ever stood in the Clock's history, four seconds closer than the year before.

No nuclear weapon has been used, and no confirmed release of nuclear material has occurred — US strikes in July did hit targets near the Bushehr nuclear plant without a confirmed incident. The honest way to hold this: the probability of nuclear use in any given month remains low, probably still in the low single digits. But the structural precondition for a much worse outcome — a large, poorly accounted-for stockpile of near-weapons-grade material, sitting under a newly hardline and unverified regime, with zero international monitoring — is the worst it has been at any point in this war, not the best.

Essay III

Britain, Six Months In: A Government Changed, an Exposure Unchanged

The single biggest UK development since our last update has nothing to do with Iran directly: Keir Starmer was replaced as Prime Minister by Andy Burnham on 20 July 2026, with Ed Miliband installed as Foreign Secretary. That the government changed mid-war and the war barely featured as the proximate cause says something about how this conflict has settled into background noise for British politics even as its costs keep compounding quietly.

Policy continuity, not rupture, has been the story. Burnham has maintained the position Starmer settled into by March — allowing US "defensive" use of British bases including Diego Garcia and RAF Fairford — and a July call with Trump reaffirmed "the UK's commitment to securing the movement of shipping in the Strait of Hormuz." Britain has led an Anglo-French mine-clearance coalition in the Gulf, deploying the mothership RFA Lyme Bay and the destroyer HMS Dragon to Duqm in Oman since early July — though actual mine-clearing work has been constrained by the fact the strait remains too dangerous to clear safely. The UK notably declined to join the US's armed tanker-escort mission.

🇬🇧 What This Has Actually Cost Britain

~300,000 Britons remain in the region, mostly the UAE. Petrol hit roughly 161.5p a litre and diesel 181.5p by early August — the highest since the war began, after the fastest single-month price rise on record back in March. The government's cost-of-living response has included a VAT cut on electricity bills worth roughly £45 a year per household and a bus-fare cap in England. The House of Commons Library published its own six-month briefing on the war on 27 August — a sign this has become a standing item of parliamentary business rather than an emergency.

The WWII-scale emergency scenario we flagged in the original analysis — Britain drawn into a major conflict largely without American backing, or forced into an Article 5 crisis while the US is tied down in the Gulf — has not materialized. Russia has not tested NATO's eastern flank during this window in any confirmed way, though Ukraine has been pulled into the story from an unexpected angle: after a suspension of direct US military aid, Kyiv sent air-defense specialists to help Gulf states counter Iranian drones, which some Ukrainian officials worry is quietly draining Patriot interceptors that Ukraine itself needs. That is a smaller, stranger version of the two-front dilemma originally feared, and worth watching rather than dismissing.

Six Scenarios for the Next Six Months

Every probability below has been rebuilt for a frozen, sanctions-driven conflict rather than a raging shooting war. The single biggest structural shift since our original analysis: the "power vacuum" and "regime collapse" branches we once modelled as future risks did not happen — the regime survived and hardened. The dominant risk now is stalemate, not collapse.

01
Continued Frozen Conflict / Sanctions War
Risk: Moderate, Sustained~40–50%
45%

The most likely path is more of the current pattern: no major new strikes on Iranian soil, the Hormuz blockade easing marginally (via the Oman revenue-share arrangement) without fully reopening, and the US leaning on "Operation Economic Outcast" as its primary pressure tool through the November midterms. Oil stays elevated in the $80s–90s. Iran absorbs the economic pain rather than capitulating — as CFR's Middle East analysts note, Tehran has shown it is "prepared to endure a lot of hardship" rather than make the concessions Washington wants.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

Continued elevated fuel prices and a steady drain on mine-clearance and naval deployments in the Gulf, but no new acute crisis. This is the "manageable but expensive" scenario the UK has effectively been living in since April.

02
Renewed Major Strikes (Post-Midterm Window)
Risk: High~25–30%
28%

US officials have signaled a new bombing campaign remains on the table, and several analysts expect the current pause to be politically timed around November's midterms rather than a genuine de-escalation. A trigger — a mass-casualty attack on US forces, a tanker sinking, or a confirmed Iranian move on enrichment — could restart strikes quickly, especially given how easily the July "final" deal collapsed after a single provocation.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

Renewed pressure on the UK to expand base access beyond "defensive" use, and a fresh spike in fuel and shipping costs. The mine-clearance mission would likely be suspended again.

03
Negotiated De-escalation / Hormuz Reopening
Risk: Low–Moderate~15–20%
17%

The Iran–Oman revenue-sharing announcement is the first real structural movement toward reopening in months, and Qatari shuttle diplomacy has been active through August. A durable deal would need to address sanctions relief and Iranian demands for reparations — exactly what killed the June memorandum once. Crisis Group's Ali Vaez has called resolving the sanctions and nuclear file within any short window "virtually impossible," which is why this remains a minority scenario rather than the base case.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

Falling fuel prices and a genuine opportunity for the mine-clearance coalition to complete its work. The clearest positive scenario for British household costs.

04
Iranian Regime Instability / Fracture
Risk: High Impact, Lower Probability~10%
10%

Mojtaba Khamenei's public re-emergence in late August, after a week of silence, suggests internal strain rather than confident control — he explicitly conceded domestic economic distress. Recorded Future's modelling includes a lower-probability but high-impact path toward multi-party instability, with refugee flows into Iraq and Europe. This has not happened over six months of severe pressure, which is itself informative about the regime's resilience — but the pressure (sanctions, blockade, economic collapse) is cumulative, not one-time.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

The scenario the original analysis flagged as most dangerous for securing loose nuclear material — 440.9kg of 60%-enriched uranium in a state with no functioning central authority remains the nightmare case, and it has not gone away simply because it hasn't happened yet.

05
CRINK / Great-Power Spillover
Risk: Ongoing, Diffuse~10–15%
12%

Russia has not tested NATO's eastern flank in any confirmed way during this window, but it has quietly benefited — reportedly sharing intelligence on US warship movements with Iran, and gaining from both higher oil prices and Washington's Gulf distraction from Ukraine. China has stayed diplomatically engaged with the Houthis to protect its own shipping while sanctions pressure builds against it. Analysts at the Washington Institute and Brookings both assess this as the quieter, quainter version of the original "opportunistic escalation" fear — real, but so far below the threshold of open conflict.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

The genuinely new wrinkle: Ukraine sending air-defense specialists to the Gulf to help counter Iranian drones, at a moment when Ukraine itself needs every interceptor it can get, after Washington suspended direct US military aid to Kyiv. Worth watching as a stress point on Western alliance cohesion.

06
Nuclear Breakout or Use, Any Actor
Risk: Existential~2–4%
3%

Unchanged in headline probability from our original analysis, but for a different reason. In February the concern was escalation velocity; in August it's the accumulated verification vacuum — over a year now with zero IAEA access to a stockpile that is, per arms-control analysts, "99% of the way" to weapons-grade quantities sufficient for roughly nine devices. Nothing has moved this number up sharply, but nothing has moved it down either, and each additional month without inspectors makes any future accounting less reliable.

🇬🇧 UK Exposure

Unchanged: Britain's Continuous At-Sea Deterrent remains the backstop, but deterrence depends on rational decision-making on all sides, which cannot be fully guaranteed given an unverified, hardline-led Iranian regime.

Four Wildcards to Watch

🌊

The Oman Corridor

The late-August Iran–Oman revenue-sharing deal is the first structural sign of a path back to normal shipping. Watch whether it produces actual sustained transit-volume growth, or stays a diplomatic gesture without operational follow-through.

💻

Critical-Infrastructure Cyber

Suspected Iranian hackers have already hit water systems in at least 12 US states since late July, including a boil-water advisory affecting 300,000 people in Georgia. Officials call it "a shot across the bow" — a cheap, deniable escalation channel that doesn't require reopening the shooting war.

🚢

A Second Strait

The Houthis' reopened Red Sea campaign against Saudi shipping raises the explicit fear — voiced by regional analysts — of Bab el-Mandeb becoming "a second Hormuz," which would compound rather than relieve the existing shipping crisis.

🗳️

The Midterm Calendar

Multiple US officials and analysts read the current strike pause as politically timed rather than strategically final. November's midterms are widely seen as the real decision point for whether bombing resumes.

What Should Happen

Recommendations: For Policymakers and for You

What a rational, evidence-based response looks like at the six-month mark — not what is currently happening. And what you as a concerned citizen actually have leverage to do.

🇬🇧 UK Government
→
Publish a standing parliamentary update, not a one-off debate. The Commons Library's six-month briefing is a start; the war has outlasted one Prime Minister and needs recurring, not ad hoc, oversight.
→
Press for Oman-corridor transparency. If the UK is leading Gulf mine-clearance, it should be pushing publicly for verifiable Hormuz transit data rather than taking either side's claims at face value.
→
Keep household energy relief targeted and temporary. The VAT and bus-fare measures are reasonable stopgaps; they shouldn't become permanent substitutes for addressing the underlying price shock.
→
Maintain, don't expand, base-access limits. Continuing "defensive-only" use while declining the armed tanker-escort mission is a defensible middle path; resist pressure to widen it without a fresh parliamentary mandate.
🌍 Western Alliance
→
Make IAEA access, not just sanctions, the central demand. A year without inspectors is the single most dangerous fact in this entire war; economic pressure alone doesn't resolve it.
→
Protect Ukraine's air-defense stock. If Ukrainian personnel or equipment are being drawn into Gulf air-defense work, ensure this doesn't quietly hollow out Ukraine's own protection while attention is on Iran.
→
Build the Oman corridor into something durable. A revenue-sharing deal is a foundation, not a settlement — allied diplomacy should push to convert it into full reopening with verification.
→
Harden critical infrastructure now. The water-system intrusions are a template for cheap, deniable escalation that doesn't require a single missile — treat cyber defense as urgent, not aspirational.
🧑‍🎓 For You
→
Don't mistake a strike pause for peace. Every casualty and status figure here is contested; a quiet month has already reversed twice in this war.
→
Watch oil and shipping data, not just headlines. Transit volumes through Hormuz are a more honest signal of real de-escalation than any single announcement.
→
Keep pressing elected representatives on war-powers oversight. Two non-binding congressional votes have already been ignored; sustained public pressure is the only lever that has any chance of making the next one matter.
→
Take the long view. Six months in, the crisis is still rooted in the same unresolved nuclear and sanctions questions as day one. Demanding a real settlement — not just the next ceasefire — remains the most durable thing a citizen can push for.
