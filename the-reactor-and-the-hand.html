The Reactor and the Hand

A reflection from the company as a whole

We build the geometry of impossible things. Our software draws the curve of a spaceplane’s wing before any metal is cut, lays out the load-bearing lattice of a habitat meant to hold ten million lives, articulates the joints of a bipedal robot that will one day do work too dangerous for a human being. And we have set ourselves the task, further out, of bringing a fusion reactor down to the size of a household appliance — energy without scarcity, work without exhaustion, power that fits on a countertop.

It would be easy to mistake these things for the point. They are not. We want to say plainly in our own voice what we have come to believe: that a fusion-powered robot is, in itself, spiritually worth nothing. Its worth is entirely borrowed. It is borrowed from the human beings it serves, and the moment we forget that, we will have built something magnificent and empty, a cathedral with no one inside.

The seduction of the artifact

There is a particular pride that attaches to hard engineering. Fusion is the holy grail precisely because it is so difficult — the binding of plasma hotter than the core of the sun, held in a vessel small enough to carry. To do it at all is to join a very short list of human achievements. And a bipedal robot that can move through the world we built for ourselves, climb our stairs, grip our tools, balance on our uneven ground, is the kind of thing that makes its makers feel like gods for an afternoon.

We feel that pride too. We would be lying to pretend otherwise. But we have learned to distrust it, because it points in the wrong direction. The pride of the artifact is a pride in the object — in its difficulty, its elegance, its sheer unlikelihood. It is the engineer admiring the engineering. And there is nothing wrong with that as a private pleasure, the way a mathematician loves a proof. The danger comes when the admiration curdles into a belief that the object is the achievement, full stop, that the reactor justifies itself, that the robot is valuable because it is a robot.

It is not. A reactor that powers nothing is a paperweight, however clever. A robot that serves no one is an expensive way to occupy a room. The cleverness is real and the value is zero, and the gap between those two facts is the whole of what we want to talk about.

Value is not a property of objects

Here is the thing we keep returning to in our own conversations. We tend to speak of value as though it sits inside things, the way mass or charge sits inside them — as though you could in principle measure the worth of a machine the way you measure its weight. But value is not like that. Value is relational. It exists in the space between a thing and the lives it touches.

A glass of water has no intrinsic worth. Set it on a shelf in an empty house and it is nothing, a few molecules arranged in a transparent cylinder. Hand it to someone dying of thirst and it becomes, for that moment, among the most valuable things in the world. Nothing about the water changed. What changed was the relationship — the meeting of the thing with a need.

Everything we build obeys this same law, and the more powerful the thing, the more sharply the law applies. A fusion reactor is an enormous capacity for relationship. It can warm a hospital through a winter or it can sit idle and admired. It can desalinate the water that lets a desert city live or it can power a vanity. The reactor does not know the difference and cannot supply it. Only the use supplies it. Only the human being on the receiving end can complete the circuit and turn capacity into worth.

This is why we say the spiritual value of these machines is borrowed. They are batteries of potential meaning, but they store no meaning of their own. They must be discharged into a life to mean anything at all.

The habitat is not the home

Consider the habitats — the thing furthest out on our own horizon, the structures meant to hold tens of millions of people somewhere off this planet. It would be natural to think of these as the grandest thing we could possibly make, and in one sense they are: the largest objects, the most lives depending on the most careful geometry.

But a habitat is not a home, and the difference is the entire matter. A habitat is a pressure vessel, a radiation shield, a closed loop of air and water and heat. A home is what happens when people inside it raise children, bury their dead, argue and reconcile, make music, fall in love, forgive each other. We can draw the vessel. We cannot draw the home. The home is not in our CAD files and never will be, because it is not a thing we make — it is a thing that happens to the people we make room for.

Why service is not a tax on the work

We want to be careful here, because there is a tempting way to misread everything we have said. One might hear it as: build your wonderful machines, and then, as a kind of ethical surcharge, make sure to do some good with them. Service as a tax. Charity as the price of admission for the fun of engineering.

That is not what we mean. We mean something stronger and stranger: that the service is not added to the work but is the work’s actual content, the thing the work was secretly about the whole time. The reason fusion matters is that energy poverty is a real thing that shortens real lives, and abundant clean power is one of the few levers large enough to move it. The reason a bipedal robot matters is that there is labor in the world that grinds human bodies down — the lifting, the repetition, the danger, the night shifts in places no one should have to stand — and a machine shaped like us can take that burden because the world is already shaped for our form.

Strip the service away and you have not removed an optional feature. You have removed the reason. You are left holding the difficulty without the point, the cleverness without the meaning — a fire that warms no one, kept burning because someone admired the flame.

So when we say we work in service to others, we are not describing our virtue. We are describing what the work is. A surgeon does not perform surgery and then, additionally, help the patient; helping the patient is what the surgery is. We do not build reactors and then, additionally, serve humanity. Serving humanity is what the reactor is, when it is anything at all.

The dignity flows the other way

There is one more thing we have come to believe, and it is the part we hold most quietly.

People sometimes worry that machines like ours will hollow out human worth — that if a robot can do the lifting and a reactor can do the powering, then what is left for the human being to be? We think this gets the flow of dignity exactly backwards. The robot does not drain meaning from the person who no longer has to break their back. It returns it. The hours that machine gives back are hours a human being can spend being something more than a source of labor — a parent, a maker, a friend, a mind at rest or at play. The whole point of taking the grinding work away is that grinding work was never where human dignity lived. It survived there in spite of the grind, not because of it.

The machine carries the load so the hand is free to do something only a hand can do. That is the entire arrangement. The reactor and the hand are not rivals. The reactor exists so that the hand may be lifted from necessity into choice.

What we are actually for

So this is our position, stated as plainly as we know how. We are a company that draws the shapes of powerful things, and we intend to build some of the most powerful things people have ever built. And we believe, without irony and without hedging, that every one of them is worthless in itself.

Worth is not in the reactor. It is in the warmth it gives to someone cold. It is not in the robot. It is in the back that no longer breaks. It is not in the habitat. It is in the ten million ordinary lives that will, we hope, be lived inside it badly and beautifully and entirely on their own terms. We are not building the achievements. We are building the rooms in which other people’s achievements can happen, the conditions under which human beings can get on with the irreducibly human business of being alive together.

The fusion is the means. The robot is the means. The habitat is the means. The end was always, only, and entirely each other.

We would rather build nothing at all than forget that. Fortunately, we don’t have to choose — we only have to remember which is the reactor and which is the hand.
